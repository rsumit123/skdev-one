You have rights. And in most cases, arrest is the exception, not the rule.

A calm, plain-language guide to what the police can and cannot do to you in India — on the road and off it — plus the honest gap between what the law says and what tends to happen on the ground. 00

If you remember nothing else

Stay calm. Be polite. Keep your hands visible. You are not required to be afraid to be respectful — and being respectful does not mean giving up your rights.

  1. An officer dealing with you should be in uniform with a visible name badge. You may politely ask for their name and rank. BNSS §36
  2. For most everyday offences (punishable up to 7 years), police must normally issue a written notice to appear, not arrest you on the spot. BNSS §35 Satender Kumar Antil · SC 2026
  3. If arrested, you have the right to know why, to call a lawyer, to have your family informed, and to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours. Art. 22
  4. You never have to offer a bribe, sign a blank paper, or confess. Silence is your right. Art. 20(3)
01

If you're driving and get stopped

The most common encounter — and the one you asked about. Here's the honest split between real powers and hard limits.

Traffic police can
  • Ask to see your driving licence, RC, insurance, and PUC certificate.
  • Issue a spot fine (challan) if you've violated a rule — but generally only an officer of ASI rank or above collects spot fines, and they must carry a challan book or e-challan machine. MV Act 1988
  • Ask you to take a breath (alcohol) test. Refusing without a valid reason can itself lead to arrest.
  • Hold your licence until a court fine is paid, if you can't pay on the spot.
  • Search your vehicle if they have a genuine, stated reason to suspect an offence.
Traffic police cannot
  • Demand documents or fine you while out of uniform or without identifying themselves.
  • Take your keys out of the vehicle, deflate your tyres, or damage the car. The Supreme Court has said this is not allowed.
  • Tow your vehicle while someone is still inside it.
  • Refuse a digital licence/RC shown from the DigiLocker or mParivahan app — these are legally valid. Rule 139 CMVR
  • Take a fine without giving you a receipt / valid challan.

Small practical notes

Digital documents count — but from the app, not your gallery. A licence or RC pulled up inside DigiLocker or mParivahan is accepted nationwide. A photo in your phone's camera roll is not. It's still wise to keep physical originals in the car as backup.

You can ask why you were stopped, calmly. If you believe a challan is wrong, you don't have to argue on the roadside — you can check and contest it later at echallan.parivahan.gov.in through the virtual traffic court.

Never pay a bribe. If an officer demands cash "to settle it," that is the moment to insist on a proper challan with a receipt. Let them keep your licence if needed — you'll get it back when the official fine is paid.

02

If you're detained or arrested

The Supreme Court has sharpened these protections a lot in the last two years. Arrest is meant to be a last resort — and if the paperwork isn't done right, the arrest itself can be held illegal.

  • You must be told the grounds of arrest — clearly, in a language you understand, and preferably in writing. Telling only your family is not enough, and a later chargesheet cannot fix a missing communication. Art. 22(1) Vihaan Kumar · SC 2025 Prabir Purkayastha · SC 2024
  • Arrest must be a genuine necessity, not a reflex. For offences up to 7 years, police should normally issue a notice first and record written reasons if they arrest. Arnesh Kumar · SC 2014 Satender Kumar Antil · SC 2026
  • You have the right to a lawyer of your choice, and to consult one during interrogation. If you can't afford one, the state must provide free legal aid.
  • Your family or a friend must be informed of the arrest and where you're held — this is not optional. D.K. Basu · SC 1997
  • An arrest memo must be made, signed by a witness and by you, noting the time and place. BNSS §36
  • You must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours (travel time excluded). Being held longer without this is illegal. Art. 22(2)
  • You have the right to stay silent. No one can force you to confess or self-incriminate. Art. 20(3)
  • You cannot be routinely handcuffed or chained — that's allowed only in exceptional cases with recorded reasons; doing it casually violates your dignity. Prem Shankar Shukla · SC 1980 Vihaan Kumar · SC 2025
  • You can ask for a medical examination at the time of arrest and again at release — a protection against custodial abuse. BNSS §53, §56
03

Bail, in plain words

Families lose their most useful days here, simply because nobody explained the map. There are four doors out of custody — in the order they usually matter.

  • Bailable offence — bail is your right, at the police station itself. The officer must release you on a bail bond; if you can't arrange a surety, an indigent person can be released on a personal bond. Nobody's permission or "mood" is involved. BNSS §478
  • Non-bailable offence — bail is the court's call, but jail is not the default. The magistrate weighs flight risk, tampering with evidence, and the Antil principle that liberty is the rule. Apply early; don't wait "for things to cool down." BNSS §480 Satender Kumar Antil · SC
  • Anticipatory bail — protection before arrest. If you genuinely fear arrest in a case (real or engineered), you can move the Sessions Court or High Court in advance for an order that police must release you on bail if they arrest. This is the tool for false-case pressure. BNSS §482
  • Default bail — the deadline the police must meet, not you. If the chargesheet isn't filed within 60 days of arrest (90 for offences punishable with death, life, or 10+ years), bail becomes your indefeasible right the moment you apply. Courts cannot refuse it. BNSS §187(3)

What family should do in the first hours

Write down three things: the FIR number, the sections applied, and the police station. That's the first thing any lawyer asks, and any delay in knowing it is pure lost time. Then call a lawyer — or 15100 for free legal aid if you can't afford one.

"Bail money" handed to a police officer is not bail. Bail is granted by a court (or at the station on a bond, with paperwork). Cash across a table with no receipt is just a bribe with a nicer name — and it buys nothing that can be enforced.

Bail is not the end of the case — conditions apply (appearing when required, not contacting witnesses). Breaking them is how bail gets cancelled.

04

If a woman is in your car

Extra safeguards apply — useful to know if you're with your family, partner, or a female friend.

The law says a woman should not be arrested between sunset and sunrise, except in exceptional circumstances — and even then, only by a woman police officer who has obtained a magistrate's written permission. BNSS §43(5)

A male officer must not physically touch a woman to make an arrest; that requires a woman officer. Her submission to arrest is presumed on a spoken intimation.

One honest caveat: some High Courts have called the night-arrest rule "directory" rather than absolute — meaning an arrest isn't automatically void if it's broken, but the officer must justify why. It's still a strong safeguard you can point to firmly and calmly. Madras HC · 2025

05

In the moment

Do

  • Stay calm and keep your voice even, even if theirs isn't.
  • Keep your hands visible; move slowly for documents.
  • Ask, politely: "May I know why I'm being stopped?"
  • Note the officer's name, rank, and badge; the time and place.
  • Record video if it's safe and you can do it openly.
  • Ask for a receipt / proper challan for any fine.

Don't

  • Argue, abuse, or raise your voice — it rarely helps.
  • Physically resist or try to drive off.
  • Offer or accept a bribe to "settle" it.
  • Sign a blank or incomplete paper.
  • Boast about contacts or "who you know."
  • Volunteer more than you're asked.
"Officer, I'd like to cooperate. May I know your name and rank, and under which rule I'm being stopped?"Calm, respectful, and it signals you know the process.
"Am I being detained, or am I free to go?"A fair question that clarifies where you actually stand.
06

On paper vs. on the ground

This is the part most guides skip. The law is one thing; a real checkpoint at night is another. None of this asks you to break a rule — it's about holding your rights in the way that actually keeps you safe in India.

New law, old habits
On paper

The BNSS and BNS replaced the CrPC and IPC on 1 July 2024, with notice-first, arrest-as-exception rules.

On the ground

Many field officers still run on old muscle memory. Quoting "Section 35 BNSS" at a constable can draw a blank look — or irritation.

Smart move

Don't lecture the law at the roadside. Comply with lawful, routine asks, note what happened, and raise the legal points later in writing or before the magistrate — where they actually carry weight.

Digital documents — valid, but often refused
On paper

A DL or RC shown from DigiLocker or mParivahan is legally valid across India.

On the ground

Some officers still insist on physical papers or say "phone won't do."

Smart move

Keep physical originals in the car anyway. Show the app, not a gallery screenshot. If wrongly refused, don't turn it into a standoff — note it and contest any challan online.

The "settle it here" pressure
On paper

Every fine needs a proper challan and receipt, and a wrong challan can be contested.

On the ground

Charges are sometimes inflated or invented to push a cash "settlement" — more so if you're alone or in a hurry.

Smart move

Don't pay cash to settle. A bribe can't be contested and marks you as an easy target next time. Insist on a real challan; let them keep your licence if needed; contest at echallan.parivahan.gov.in. The official fine is almost always the cheaper path.

"Just come to the station"
On paper

For offences up to 7 years you should get a written notice, not be picked up. The moment they confine you, arrest safeguards switch on.

On the ground

People are often "asked" to come in for questioning with no notice — an informal detention that blurs the line.

Smart move

Ask calmly: "Am I under arrest? Under which section or FIR?" You're generally not obliged to go for mere questioning without a proper notice. If you do go, tell family where you are and call a lawyer first.

Recording the encounter
On paper

No law bars filming police in a public place, and courts have protected the right to record.

On the ground

Officers may object, order you to stop, or try to grab the phone.

Smart move

Record openly but without taunting. Better: use a setting that auto-uploads to the cloud so footage survives even if the phone is taken, and let a passenger film from inside the car. Don't make the camera the fight.

When the FIR won't get registered
On paper

Anyone can report a cognizable offence, and a Zero FIR can be filed at any station regardless of jurisdiction.

On the ground

Stations sometimes refuse, deflect, or delay — especially a complaint against police.

Smart move

Insist politely and get it in writing. If refused, complain to the SP and to the Magistrate under BNSS §175(3), and in parallel to the State Human Rights Commission.

The real safety rule
On paper

You hold many rights in the moment of the encounter.

On the ground

Asserting them aggressively at a checkpoint — especially at night, alone, with no witnesses — can invite an "obstruction" charge or worse.

Smart move

Your only goal in the moment is to get home safely with a clear record. Be calm, be trackable (someone knows where you are), document quietly — and fight it on paper afterwards, where the law is strongest.

07

The traps that catch ordinary people

Most people don't get into trouble because they don't know their rights — they get into trouble in one of these six ways. Each is avoidable.

  • Losing your temper. The fastest way to turn a ₹500 challan into a criminal case. Obstructing or assaulting a public servant is a separate offence BNS §132, §221 — and unlike the challan, it follows you. Stay boring; fight on paper later.
  • Offering the bribe yourself. Since the 2018 amendment, giving a bribe is a crime too — up to 7 years PC Act §8. The only safe harbour is if you were coerced and report it within 7 days. Many people think only the taker is liable; that stopped being true years ago.
  • Unlocking your phone because they asked. There is no general power at a stop to browse your phone; its contents sit behind your right to privacy Puttaswamy · SC 2017 and the bar on self-incrimination Art. 20(3). Decline politely. If they seize the phone, that needs a proper seizure memo — don't wrestle over it, get the paper.
  • Handing over anything without a memo. Phone, vehicle, documents — any seizure needs paperwork listing what was taken, when, and by whom. No memo means it can quietly become "never happened." Ask for it every time.
  • Forwarding that WhatsApp message. Forwarding fake or inflammatory content can itself be an offence BNS §196, §353, and group admins get summoned first. If you didn't verify it, don't forward it — especially anything about communities, riots, or public figures.
  • Driving past an accident because "police will harass me." The opposite of a trap — a protection people don't use. A Good Samaritan who helps an accident victim cannot be harassed, detained, or forced to disclose their identity, and hospitals must treat first MV Act §134A. Helping is safe. The hour you save someone is the one that matters.
08

When it's not a police matter at all

A huge share of police pressure on ordinary people happens in disputes where the police have no lawful role. Their powers exist for crimes. Money and property disagreements belong to civil courts — and the Supreme Court has repeatedly deprecated using criminal machinery as a recovery weapon. Indian Oil v. NEPC · SC 2006

Not police business: unpaid loans between individuals, business or partnership fallouts, landlord–tenant disputes, property boundaries and possession, refunds and deposits, most family property fights. A bounced cheque goes to court under NI Act §138 — not to a police station.

The pressure pattern: the other side files (or threatens) a "cheating" FIR to convert a money dispute into a criminal scare, or you get a phone call to "just come to the station and settle." A phone call is not a summons, and a station-table "settlement meeting" over money has no legal basis. Sitting in that meeting rarely ends the matter; it usually prices it.

What to do: respond to paper, not phone calls. Ask for the FIR number and sections in writing; if a genuine written notice comes, appear with a lawyer. State your position once, calmly: the dispute is civil and you'll answer it in the proper forum. If officers keep leaning on you to pay, that's a written complaint to the SP — and the pressure usually stops the moment it's on record.

"If there is an FIR, please give me its number and sections — I will respond to any written notice through my lawyer."Polite, final, and it moves the dispute back onto paper, where you're safest.
09

If they mistreat you

Misconduct has real consequences now — custodial violence and death are defined offences under the new penal code. You have clear channels.

  1. Gather details on the spot. Officer's name, rank, badge number, vehicle number, station; the date, time and location. Video and photos if safe.
  2. Escalate above them. Complain to the senior officer at the police station, then to the Superintendent of Police (SP) of the district in writing.
  3. Tell the magistrate. If arrested, raise any mistreatment the first time you're produced in court — it goes on record and the magistrate must check it.
  4. File with a rights body. The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) or the Jharkhand State Human Rights Commission can be approached directly, including online.
  5. Get free legal help. The Jharkhand State Legal Services Authority (JHALSA) provides free legal aid and can guide the complaint.

10

The judgments behind this

Not just "the book" — these are the actual rulings that turned each principle into something you can hold a police officer to. Look any of them up if you want the full text.

Satender Kumar Antil v. CBI
Supreme Court · 15 Jan 2026

For offences punishable up to 7 years, a notice to appear under BNSS §35(3) is the rule and arrest is the exception. Arrest must be an objective necessity, backed by reasons recorded in writing — not a matter of convenience.

Vihaan Kumar v. State of Haryana
Supreme Court · 7 Feb 2025 · 2025 INSC 162

An arrest is illegal if the grounds aren't actually communicated to you in a language you understand. Informing a relative isn't a substitute, and a later chargesheet or remand can't cure the defect. Handcuffing and chaining a patient to a hospital bed was held to violate the right to dignity.

Prabir Purkayastha v. State (NCT of Delhi)
Supreme Court · 15 May 2024

Grounds of arrest must be furnished in writing. The court drew a clear line between "grounds" — the specific facts against you — and generic "reasons," and held that a person is entitled to the former so they can actually defend themselves.

Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India
Supreme Court · 2023

The origin of the written-grounds rule (in a money-laundering context): oral communication of the grounds of arrest isn't enough to satisfy the constitutional safeguard.

Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar
Supreme Court · 2014

No automatic, mechanical arrest for offences punishable up to 7 years. Police must first justify whether an arrest is even necessary, and issue a notice of appearance where it isn't.

Indian Oil Corporation v. NEPC India Ltd.
Supreme Court · 2006

Criminal prosecution is not a shortcut for civil remedies. The court deprecated the growing tendency to give a criminal colour to civil disputes — money, contracts, property — to pressure the other side, calling it an abuse of the process of law.

D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal
Supreme Court · 1997

The custody rulebook: an arrest memo signed by a witness, informing a family member or friend, a medical examination, and clearly identifiable officers. These are still the backbone of what the police must do.

Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India
Supreme Court · 2017 · Nine-judge bench

Privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21. This is the constitutional footing for treating the contents of your phone as protected — the state needs a lawful basis and proportionate procedure to get into it, not mere curiosity at a checkpoint.

Prem Shankar Shukla v. Delhi Administration
Supreme Court · 1980

Routine handcuffing is unconstitutional and an affront to dignity. It's permitted only in genuinely exceptional cases, with reasons — a principle the court reaffirmed in 2025.

Supreme Court on §35 notice service
Supreme Court · 2025

A notice under BNSS §35 must be served through a proper legal mode. A WhatsApp or other electronic message alone is not valid service.

Tavaragi Rajashekhar Shiva Prasad v. State of Karnataka
Karnataka High Court

A §35 notice must mention the crime number and offence and attach a copy of the FIR. If it doesn't, you aren't obliged to appear and no coercive action can follow for non-appearance.

Keep this ready

Five minutes now saves a lot of stress later. A quick check for your car and phone.

  • Physical originals in the car: DL, RC, insurance, PUC.
  • Same documents added to DigiLocker and mParivahan on your phone.
  • A saved note with a lawyer's number, two family contacts, and your blood group.
  • Phone kept charged (or a power bank) so you can show documents and record if needed.
  • A recording app set to auto-upload to the cloud, so footage is safe even if the phone isn't.
  • This page bookmarked — a two-minute read whenever you want the ground back under your feet.

A note on this guide. This is general information to help you understand your rights, not legal advice for a specific situation. India's criminal laws changed on 1 July 2024 (the BNSS and BNS replaced the CrPC and IPC), and enforcement varies between states and officers — Jharkhand and local police rules may add their own procedures, and some points above (like the traffic-rank convention) rest on long-standing practice and advisories rather than a single statute. For anything serious, or an actual case, speak to a lawyer or reach JHALSA on 15100. If in immediate danger, call 112. Your safety in the moment matters more than winning an argument on the roadside.
